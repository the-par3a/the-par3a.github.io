A NEW WORLD · 2042

Royesh: Beyond the Sky's Memory

Eighteen months after Royesh was founded, an unexplained phenomenon has emerged in the sky and in the memories of its residents. Every night, a star vanishes from a fixed point; every morning, someone remembers something that never happened.

Through which lens will you approach this mystery? Your choice changes your starting perspective—not what awaits the city.

These choices are not personality labels; they are four possible entry points. Another perspective may become more convincing as the story unfolds.

Royesh: The City the Sky Cannot Remember

Royesh, 2042. From a distance, the city looks like a fresh blueprint rising from the earth: straight streets, uniform blocks, and a glass building intended to become its administrative center. Up close, the map's geometric order has given way to life—unpainted walls, shops thriving before the sidewalks are finished, and people who each see this place differently.

Eighteen months have passed since the city was founded, and nearly eight thousand people live here. For the past seventeen nights, the digital clocks downtown have stopped—not switched off, but frozen—for exactly seventy-three seconds at 03:17. During that interval, the cameras record the same image: an empty street beneath a sky whose star pattern matches no recorded date. Stranger still, several residents have spoken the next morning of walking along that very street—a street that does not exist in the city.

At first, the council considered the disruptions a technical problem. But when the observatory reported that, at 03:17, starlight in the raw data formed a pattern resembling Royesh's street map, the matter went beyond a technical fault. The pattern grew more complete every night, as though something above were rereading the city's blueprint with impossible precision.

A small team of researchers, engineers, and residents has been tasked with investigating these events before the council evacuates the city or conceals the phenomenon. You have joined them. The official mission is simple: determine whether you are dealing with faulty instruments or a phenomenon for which you do not yet have the language.

If the sky knew the city before it was built, then what exactly has been built on this plain? And why do some residents remember a place that does not yet exist?

Chapter One: The Street on No Map

It was 03:17. For seventy-three seconds, the city stopped moving—or at least every device that recorded time said it did.

In the observatory control room, the city map glowed on the main display. Beside it was an image of the previous night's sky, where the stars had formed orderly lines instead of their natural scatter. The lines traced Royesh's streets—even an alley that had not yet been excavated. Dr. Sohrabi enlarged the image. “This match is no coincidence. But we still don't know what we're looking at.”

The first hypothesis was a simultaneous fault in the timekeeping system and the cameras. But mechanical clocks, independent devices, and even a weather station outside the city all confirmed the same seventy-three-second pause. No one could explain why a local disruption had also been recorded beyond the city's limits.

Leila, a young astrophysics student working at the observatory, placed a folder on the table. Seven residents, independently of one another, had described a similar dream: a narrow street with blue lights, a windowless building, and a voice repeating the city's name from the end of a dark tunnel. The details differed, but all shared one feature: in the dream, the city was seen from above, arranged exactly as the stars appeared.

“A memory alone is not proof that a place exists,” Dr. Sohrabi said. “But when independent accounts overlap with a pattern in our astronomical data, we cannot simply dismiss them.”

Naderi, the observatory's elderly caretaker, stared at the image. He said he had seen the same pattern in an old photograph twenty years earlier, in another city—a photograph that vanished from a research center's archive after an earthquake. He had no evidence to support his claim. The only thing he remembered was the title written on the back: “The Threshold Map.”

Leila pointed to the raw data. “This map doesn't merely resemble our city. A new street is added every night—a street we haven't built yet. It's as if the image is moving ahead of us.”

In the room's silence, the fans seemed louder. A new line appeared at the edge of the map, unconnected to any existing street. Then a short name appeared beneath it: “Return Square.” No one had typed it. The observatory system was not connected to the municipal network.

Near dawn, you and Leila went to the coordinates of the new line. It was an empty plot: no buildings, lights, or signs. Yet a handheld compass turned toward the center of the plot with every step. Leila's audio recording captured the wind—and, in the background, a quiet woman's voice saying your name. Leila had not heard it. The file carried a timestamp from seventy-three seconds earlier.

The team decided to document events without rushing to interpret them: clock stoppages, changes in the star map, dream accounts, and anything observed at the new coordinates. No one would visit the site alone, and raw data would be stored on several independent systems.

What appeared in the sky might not have been a map. It might have been a memory of something that had not happened yet.

Decision One · Where Should We Look First?

Which clue should the team investigate first?

Some residents remember a street that is not there. The instruments record a 73-second pause. Both clues matter—but the team must choose where to begin.

At first, the accounts seemed too inconsistent to trust. Then the team noticed one detail repeated by people who had never met: a narrow doorway at the end of the missing street. That same shape had appeared in the latest star map. No one could explain how they knew it.
The devices passed their basic checks. But their records disagreed in a way no ordinary fault could explain: while the clocks showed a seventy-three-second pause, several independent systems had captured data from that very interval. The gap was not empty. Something had been recorded inside it.

Chapter Two: A Voice from the Other Side of Time

Three nights later, at 03:17, the observatory's silent loudspeaker switched on. A crackling but clear voice delivered a short message: “If you can hear this message, you are still in the first city.” Then several people could be heard talking. One voice belonged to Leila. She was standing beside you, staring at the speaker in alarm.

Dr. Sohrabi proposed installing an independent instrument package at the new coordinates: magnetic sensors, a portable atomic clock, and cameras with no network connection. The goal was not to hunt for a creature; they wanted to find out whether that location altered the rules of measurement. Leila had another suggestion: replay the message and see whether a new response arrived.

Leila spoke quietly. “If that voice really comes from the future, any answer we give might create the very thing we're trying to escape. But if we don't answer, how will we know what's waiting on the other side of the message?”

“I heard my own voice in the recording, too,” Naderi said. “But it said things I never said. It spoke of a city that wasn't called Royesh—a city where they said the gate had to be closed before sunrise.”

Dr. Sohrabi insisted that no one should present the recording as definitive proof of communication with the future. “Voice similarity can be a perceptual error. Timestamps can be manipulated. But even after considering all that, we still have a phenomenon for which we have no ordinary explanation.”

The investigation protocol was written: instruments must be independent; no data may be overwritten; every event must be recorded from multiple angles; personal accounts must be kept separate from physical observations; and no one may be alone during the disruption. If the message played again, the team would ask only one prearranged question: “Where are you?”

Disagreement spread through the team. Some wanted to evacuate the site; others believed the phenomenon might be harmless and could even open a door to new knowledge. Leila spoke less than anyone. She had replayed the recording many times and said that, at its end, she could hear her own voice whispering: “This time, don't open the door.”

At the end of the meeting, Dr. Sohrabi said, “Perhaps the right question isn't what lies on the other side. Perhaps we should first ask why something over there knows the shape of our city.”

“The unknown is not merely something we have yet to explain; sometimes, explaining it changes the very problem itself.”

Decision Two · Should We Reply?

A voice from the recording seems to know the city—and the people in the room. What should the team do?

A reply might give the team a clue. But they do not know who—or what—is on the other side.

The team asked only once: “Where are you?” Static filled the room. Then came a reply: “In the same place you will be.” A second voice followed—Leila's, but colder and strangely distant: “We haven't built you yet.” Leila, standing beside the speaker, insisted she had said nothing.
The team made no reply. Instead, they compared independent readings from the site. The magnetic field shifted without a known source, the soil appeared ordinary, and the portable atomic clock ran exactly seventy-three seconds ahead of the reference. The evidence grew stronger; the explanation did not.

Between Science and the Unknown

Science is not the enemy of the unknown; it is the enemy of unsupported claims. The absence of an explanation does not give us permission to invent any explanation we like. We must distinguish observation from interpretation, and possibility from fact.

A memory can be a clue, not a final verdict. An instrument can record data, but it cannot decide what that data means. Even the most precise measurements may do no more than reveal the boundaries of our ignorance when faced with an unknown phenomenon.

Clear thinking in the face of mystery does not mean silencing the imagination; it means not allowing imagination to take the place of evidence. We can ask about seemingly impossible possibilities without calling the first answer the truth.

Perhaps the greatest danger is not the unknown itself, but the certainty we develop before we understand it.

Chapter Three: A City on the Other Side of the Mirror

On the twenty-first night, the team gathered at the coordinates. No one called the place “Return Square” aloud. The atomic clock, cameras, and sensors were running. The sky was clear, yet the display showed a star invisible to the naked eye. At 03:16:59, every device suddenly recorded the same number: 00:00:00.

For seventy-three seconds, all ambient sound vanished. The microphones recorded no wind, rustling clothes, or breathing. But the cameras kept recording. Their footage showed the team looking through glass at a city exactly like Royesh, with the same buildings, streets, and observatory. Only its sky was empty, and every house had illuminated windows.

When time resumed, a new image appeared on the displays. The city they had seen did not exist on any official map; in the image, the observatory had not yet been built. In its place stood a circular structure, a faint light shining skyward from its center. Leila enlarged the image. A group stood behind a window in one of the buildings. One of them raised a hand at the exact moment Leila lowered hers.

The data suggested that time flowed differently on the other side of the image—or at least seemed to. An hour later, an audio file appeared in the system with a timestamp three days in the future. Dr. Sohrabi's voice said: “If you're hearing this, do not look at the second image. Under any circumstances.”

Leila did not open the second image. But it appeared on the display without anyone touching it: the same city, now seen from higher up. The circular structure glowed at its center, and the streets reached toward it like narrow veins. Along the edge of the image, a sentence appeared: “The first city was a prototype.”

Dr. Sohrabi suggested that the image might be a reflection of their own data, generated by an algorithm using the city's maps and photographs. But the observatory had no generative model, and the image had been recorded in the offline memory of a camera that only captured footage. No one could explain how the sentence had been added to the file.

On the final night of the investigation, the team split in two. Half stayed at the observatory; the rest remained at the coordinates. At 03:17, no new star went dark. Instead, every recorded star aligned into a single pattern for seventy-three seconds. Royesh's streets were completed on the sky map, and a bright point appeared at their center. Then Leila's recorded voice played through the speaker: “This time, don't open the door.”

The team could not reach a definitive conclusion. There was no evidence that a parallel world existed, yet the technical-failure hypothesis did not fit all the data either. The final report left three possibilities open: an unknown measurement anomaly, a natural but unfamiliar phenomenon, or an event beyond the explanatory reach of current science. None had been proven.

A council member asked, “So what was on the other side, after all?”

Dr. Sohrabi replied, “We don't know. And if anyone now claims to know, we should ask what evidence supports that claim. All we know is that something was recorded in the data, and we still cannot explain it.”

That night, for the first time in seventeen days, the clocks did not stop at 03:17. But the observatory's dark display reflected the room where everyone stood—except one person. Your place in the image was empty. On the other side of the glass, someone had written in the condensation: “Welcome home.”

Decision Three · What Should We Tell the City?

The evidence is still incomplete, but the city wants answers. What should the team do next?

The team must decide how to continue—and how much to share—without claiming to know more than the evidence shows.

The team continued with independent instruments and strict safeguards. No one answered the voice again. The site remained restricted while the records were checked and stored separately. Progress was slower, but each result could be tested again.
The complete report went public: raw measurements, unexplained sounds, conflicting accounts, and every failed hypothesis. Other research teams began reviewing it. For the first time, Royesh was no longer investigating alone—but the mystery had not become any smaller.

Royesh was not built around a single answer; it took shape as its questions became more precise. The map was revised, a temporary plan was tested against real life, a calculation error was uncovered, and a report was published without hiding what remained unfinished.

Leila never spoke again about the voice that had said her name. Dr. Sohrabi sent the data to several independent centers. Naderi reconstructed the old photograph from memory; the same geometric symbol appeared on its back. No one knew where the photograph had come from, or why Royesh's name had been written on it.

Perhaps clear thinking in the face of the unknown means standing between two temptations: denying what we cannot yet explain, and believing what we do not yet have evidence for. We can face a mystery without turning it into an unquestionable legend.

On the final page of the logbook, someone found a sentence in unfamiliar handwriting: “If the sky ever completes the city's map, do not look at its center.” Beneath it was tomorrow's date.

Chapter Four: Tomorrow's Date

The next morning, the city remembered that it had not yet been born.

At 7:12 a.m., Royesh's registry office received a report dated the following day. It had come neither from the observatory nor the city network, but from an administrative office that, according to the official map, had not yet been built. Every team member was listed as a recipient. Beside Leila's name were the words “Present at the previous meeting”—a meeting none of them remembered.

Dr. Sohrabi opened the file on the display. The message was brief: “Phase One has been completed successfully. Local memory has been stabilized. An observer must be present for Phase Two to begin.” There was no signature at the bottom of the page, only coordinates matching the geometric center of Royesh's map.

Naderi, silent until then, said, “I know these coordinates.” He took a photograph from the inside pocket of his coat: an old picture of the very land on which the city had been built. In the background, before the first street had even been excavated, stood a circular building. The building was absent from the modern version of the photograph. In the old one, someone's shadow stood beside its door.

Leila enlarged the image. The person's face was unclear, but their right hand rested against the glass—exactly where the control room's reflection appeared in the new image. She asked quietly, “When was this photograph taken?”

Naderi did not answer. He was looking at the back of the photograph, where faint pencil marks read: “For the day the city recognizes its own observer.”

At that moment, the display went dark. Its black glass reflected an empty room instead of the people present—a room with a window facing a bright sky. On its desk lay a copy of the same photograph. A hand reached in from beyond the frame, picked it up, and wrote something on its back.

“Phase Two begins the moment the reader remembers this sentence.”

No one spoke. For the first time, the question was no longer merely what the city was hiding; it was whether the observer stood outside this story, or had been part of it from the beginning. Sohrabi did not close the file. Naderi did not turn the photograph over. Leila watched the glass, waiting for the reflection to make its move one beat after hers.

In the logbook, tomorrow's date had vanished—but the indentation of the handwriting remained.

About the Persian names Meaning & pronunciation

The names in this story come from Persian and the wider region. They are kept in their original form to preserve the characters’ cultural identity. Here is a little context, if you are curious.

Rooyesh — the story’s name

رویش is a Persian word for the act of growing or sprouting. It can suggest the first signs of life, the beginning of something new, or something slowly taking shape. It fits a city that is still becoming what it will be—and a mystery that reveals itself bit by bit.

Say it: roo-YESH IPA: /ruːˈjeʃ/

Leila

Used in Persian and many other languages, Leila comes from the Arabic name Layla, linked to the word for “night.” The connection feels at home in a story of stars, darkness, and hidden memories.

Say it: lay-LAH Persian IPA: /lejˈlɑː/

Sohrabi

Sohrabi is a Persian family name formed from Sohrab, a famous figure in the Shahnameh, the Persian epic poem. Keeping the family name intact preserves that cultural reference without turning it into an English label.

Say it: soh-rah-BEE Persian IPA: /sohɾɒːˈbiː/

Naderi

Naderi is a Persian family name based on Nader, a name from Arabic nādir, meaning “rare” or “uncommon.” As with the other names, its original form is kept rather than translated.

Say it: nah-deh-REE Persian IPA: /nɒːdeˈɾiː/

IPA is a standard system for writing speech sounds. The simple guides above are meant to be easier to read; the symbols after “IPA” give a more exact guide to the Persian pronunciation.
